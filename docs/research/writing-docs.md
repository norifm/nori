# Docs work when every sentence carries a fact

Excellent documentation comes from three decisions made before any prose: who the reader is, what task or question brought them to the page, and which of four page types (tutorial, how-to guide, reference, explanation) the page is. After that, the page is shaped for scanning, because **79% of web users scan instead of reading word by word, and they read at most 20 to 28% of a page's words** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)). The point goes first, headings and list items start with their meaningful words, each page has one purpose and stands alone when someone lands on it from search, and links name where they lead. AI-written docs fail less on vocabulary than on substance. Readers judge "slop" mostly by **low relevance, low information density and wrong tone**, and LLM judges asked to spot it score near chance ([Shaib et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19163v2)). The worst defects are invented commands, thoroughness on the easy parts with silence on the hard ones, one fact repeated in several places, and drifting terms. So a docs-writing skill has to combine writing rules with verification against the source and mechanical checks. A word list alone is not enough. ASD-STE100 is useful only in part. Its one-term-per-concept rule, its sentence-length caps (used as review flags) and its one instruction per step transfer well. Its approved dictionary and synonym ban do not suit software docs. User pages should describe what a feature does for the reader and link one level down to a "how it works" page, never into a chain of three or more levels.

## Readers see two words per line and arrive from search

The evidence on reading behavior is old but consistent, and it sets the shape of every page. In NN/g's 1997 study, a concise version of a page tested 58% better than a promotional control version, a scannable layout 47% better, objective language 27% better, and **all three together 124% better** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)). Later log data showed that time on a page grows by only about 4.4 seconds per 100 extra words. So an added sentence has roughly an 18% chance of being read, and **readers read half of a page only when it has 111 words or fewer** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)). That study had only 25 well-educated users, so treat the exact numbers as moderate evidence. The direction is clear, though: every sentence beyond the core has a cost.

How the eye moves depends on the cues the page gives it. With little formatting, readers fall into the F-pattern. They read the first lines and the first few words on the left of each line, and they miss the rest. NN/g calls this "the default pattern when there are no strong cues" and "bad for users and businesses" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). When headings are meaningful and match what sits under them, readers use the layer-cake pattern instead. They jump from heading to heading, "by far the most effective way to scan pages" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)). Two smaller findings matter for wording. Readers "see about 2 words for most list items", and a link starting with a generic word like "Introducing" got no correct predictions at all ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/)). Readers also skip the opening words of list items that all begin the same way (the "bypassing" pattern) ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). Front-loading is therefore a rule for every heading, list item and link, not a stylistic preference.

The page also has to work as a first page. Mark Baker's *Every Page is Page One* starts from the observation that readers "may enter a content set at any point," often from search, and that they skip around even in material written to be read in order. Each page must therefore be self-contained, have one specific purpose, set its own context, follow its type's pattern, stay on one level of abstraction, assume a qualified reader, and link richly ([Baker](https://mbakeranalecta.github.io/spfe-open-toolkit/spfe-docs-essays/every-page-is-page-one.html)). The inverted pyramid gives the order inside the page: the most important fact first, then details from broad interest to niche, so that someone who reads one paragraph still gets the point ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/)). Information-scent research adds the rule for links. A reader picks a link by estimating its value from the label, and "Learn more" has almost no scent ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/)). Google's style guide states it as a writing rule: link text is a descriptive phrase that makes sense on its own, matches or describes the target's title, and is never "here" or a bare URL, because screen-reader users jump from link to link ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/link-text)).

## Four page types settle what each sentence may say

Diátaxis gives the most usable test for "what belongs on this page." Two questions sort any page, or any sentence: does it inform action or cognition, and does it serve study or work? Action plus study is a tutorial, action plus work a how-to guide, cognition plus work reference, and cognition plus study explanation ([Diátaxis compass](https://diataxis.fr/compass/)). Django's docs landing page states the same four-way split to its readers. It is the clearest public example of a site telling people where to start ([Django](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/5.1/)). MDN uses a related three-way split into reference, guide and navigation pages, each with a template ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/MDN/Writing_guidelines/Page_structures/Page_types)).

| Page type | Reader's state | What it contains | What it must not contain | Title form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tutorial | Learning by doing | A stated destination, steps with visible results, expected output, "Notice that…" | Explanation beyond one sentence, options, alternatives | "Build your first plugin" |
| How-to guide | Competent, has a goal | Conditional imperatives ("If you want x, do y"), prerequisites first | Teaching, digressions, full option lists | "How to set up remote control" |
| Reference | Looking something up | Neutral description that mirrors the product's structure, firm warnings | Instructions, opinions, reasoning | The thing's name: "Settings: Sound" |
| Explanation | Studying, away from the product | Context, design decisions, alternatives, opinion, analogy | Steps, exhaustive parameter description | Reads after "About": "How AutoMix picks a transition" |

The table condenses the four Diátaxis type pages ([tutorials](https://diataxis.fr/tutorials/), [how-to guides](https://diataxis.fr/how-to-guides/), [reference](https://diataxis.fr/reference/), [explanation](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)). The title rule is the one writers break most. "How to integrate application performance monitoring" is good. "Integrating application performance monitoring" is ambiguous, and "Application performance monitoring" could be about what the thing is ([Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/how-to-guides/)). The common mixing errors follow from the boundaries: explanation inside a tutorial, option lists inside a how-to guide, instructions inside reference, and a "getting started" page that is both a tutorial and a feature tour. The fix is the same each time. Cut the sentence that belongs to another type, and link to the page where it belongs. Diátaxis also warns against applying itself as scaffolding. Creating four empty top-level folders is "horrible". Instead, improve one page at a time against its type and publish each improvement right away ([Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/how-to-use-diataxis/)).

John Carroll's minimalism adds what the type system leaves out: error recovery. His "paradox of sense-making" is that people act on their own mental models and skip ahead. The least experienced learners used the manuals least. So docs should let readers act at once, on real tasks, and "support error recognition and recovery" at the point where errors happen ([Baker on minimalism](https://everypageispageone.com/2013/07/02/what-is-minimalism)). Baker warns that brevity alone produces "brief systematic instruction," which readers ignore when their mental model disagrees with it. In practice, a step that can go wrong gets its "If you see X, do Y" right after it, recovery information sits next to the action it concerns ([Glasgow handout](https://www.psy.gla.ac.uk/~steve/MinMan2.html)), and every procedure shows what success looks like so the reader can tell when it failed. The Rust book does this well: it prints the real compiler error (`error[E0382]: borrow of moved value`) for code that fails ([Rust book](https://doc.rust-lang.org/book/ch04-01-what-is-ownership.html)).

## User pages describe effects and hand off one level down

Praised consumer help pages share one pattern. Obsidian's "Daily notes" opens with a definition by effect ("opens a note based on today's date, or creates it if it doesn't exist"). It describes each option by what changes when you set it, gives UI paths as prose with bold labels, keeps caveats in one tip callout, and links related concepts inline ([Obsidian Help](https://obsidian.md/help/Plugins/Daily+notes)). Linear's "Triage" opens with one line of purpose, describes settings by their visible result ("Triage will appear under the team name in the sidebar"), uses notes only for plan requirements and overrides, and ends with a collapsible FAQ ([Linear](https://linear.app/docs/triage)). Things shows how one page can serve two audiences. Everyday use comes first, precise reference after, and the material for "pro users and developers" last ([Things](https://culturedcode.com/things/support/articles/2803573/)). None of these pages explains implementation in the body. A user page answers "what does this do for me, how do I turn it on, what does it cost." The mechanism is one link away.

That link is where progressive disclosure applies. NN/g's research is on interfaces, but the principle carries over. Showing something first tells readers it is important, the path to the deeper level must be obvious, its label must set expectations, and **"designs that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability"** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)). For docs, that suggests a feature page linking to one "How X works" explanation page, and that page linking to source or developer docs. A feature page should not lead into a topic page, then a subtopic page, then the detail. Applying the two-level limit to docs is an inference, not a measured result. Its link text follows Google's rule, for example "How AutoMix chooses a transition", never "Learn more" ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/link-text)). The explanation page, following Diátaxis, may weigh alternatives and give the reasons for a design ([Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)). Tailwind's habit of naming an objection as a heading and answering it ("Why not just use inline styles?") is the model for features users question ([Tailwind](https://tailwindcss.com/docs/styling-with-utility-classes)).

Put together, a feature page has this shape. It opens with one sentence on what the feature does for the reader. Then it states which clients and versions have it, how to turn it on (exact labels in bold, per client where they differ), each option as its name plus what changes, the cost (battery, data, a server requirement), what the feature does not do, and one descriptive link to the explanation. This matches nori's own rule that settings say only what is not obvious from their name. GitLab supplies the rules for keeping such pages true. Describe the product as it is today, with no "currently" and no "new". Never promise future releases. Write from the user's side ("You can add a file," not "This feature allows you to…") ([GitLab word list](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/styleguide/word_list/); [GitLab style guide](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/styleguide/)). Write the Docs adds the reason it matters: "Consider incorrect documentation to be worse than missing documentation" ([Write the Docs](https://www.writethedocs.org/guide/writing/docs-principles/)).

## Architecture docs follow the data and name what is absent

The best "how the system works" pages begin with one sentence that models the whole system. SQLite "works by compiling SQL text into bytecode, then running that bytecode using a virtual machine." Its headings then follow the data path (Tokenizer, Parser, Code Generator, Bytecode Engine, B-Tree, Page Cache, OS Interface). Each component gets its role in one line, the design choices with their reasons ("it can be made threadsafe and it runs faster"), and the files that implement it ([SQLite](https://www.sqlite.org/arch.html)). Home Assistant's "big picture" opens "Home Assistant is not one program. It is a handful of pieces that work together." It shows a stacked diagram, names the one key abstraction (integrations turn everything into entities), and **traces one request end to end**, a light turned on from a dashboard ([Home Assistant](https://contributors.home-assistant.io/architecture/big-picture/)). Those three moves make a strong pattern: a one-sentence model, a context picture of the system with its users and neighbors (C4's system-context level, "recommended for all software development teams" and meant for "everybody, both technical and non-technical" ([C4](https://c4model.com/diagrams/system-context))), and one scenario traced through every layer. For a music player the obvious trace is a tap on a song, through the core, the engine, the decoder and the sound chain, to the output.

For the repository's own architecture doc, matklad's ARCHITECTURE.md essay fits a codebase of this size best. Keep it short, because "every recurring contributor will have to read it." Include only things "unlikely to frequently change," and revisit it a couple of times a year instead of syncing it with every commit. Its parts are a bird's-eye view, a codemap ("a map of a country, not an atlas"), architectural invariants, boundaries and cross-cutting concerns ([matklad](https://matklad.github.io/2021/02/06/ARCHITECTURE.md.html)). Two details stand out. Invariants matter most when they state an absence, because "good boundaries have measure zero" and cannot be found by reading code. And files and types are named, not linked, since links rot and readers have symbol search. rust-analyzer shows the format in practice, with bold "**Architecture Invariant:**" lines such as "parsing never fails" and the statement that the analyzer "never does any IO" ([rust-analyzer](https://github.com/rust-lang/rust-analyzer/blob/master/docs/book/src/contributing/architecture.md)). nori's AGENTS.md already holds a codemap (Layout) and boundaries. The gap an architecture doc fills is the end-to-end trace and invariants written as such, for example "Nothing on the audio path allocates" or "The core returns no user-facing text." arc42's twelve sections, including runtime view, crosscutting concepts, decisions and glossary, are a checklist to borrow headings from. Its "lean" level fits; filling in the full template does not ([arc42](https://docs.arc42.org/home/)).

### Plugin docs need three layers: first plugin, capabilities, reference

Extension docs that developers praise share one structure. VS Code separates Get Started (a Hello World that teaches the basic concepts), Extension Capabilities ("dissects VS Code's vast API into smaller categories and points you to more detailed topics"), guides, UX guidelines, and an exhaustive reference ([VS Code](https://code.visualstudio.com/api)). Obsidian's "Build a plugin" adds the safety warning early ("never develop plugins in your main vault"), gives short prerequisites, and has five numbered steps with tips for hot reloading and a reminder to restart after manifest changes ([Obsidian dev docs](https://docs.obsidian.md/Plugins/Getting+started/Build+a+plugin)). Zed is the closest analog to a Rust/wasm plugin system. It opens with a one-sentence definition ("Zed extensions are Git repositories containing an `extension.toml` manifest"), lists what an extension can provide with links, shows a sample manifest and a full directory tree, and covers the wasm target, dev install, where logs go, and the fact that a dev extension overrides an installed one ([Zed](https://zed.dev/docs/extensions/developing-extensions)). The pattern for a plugin guide is therefore: one-sentence definition, a list of extension points linking to each, a first-plugin tutorial that tells the author to work in a test profile, the manifest and tree, dev install and logs, and publishing on its own page. The things authors get stuck on, such as the reload cycle, where logs go, and what overrides what, deserve more space than the happy path.

## Sentence rules: the guides agree, and STE helps only in part

Google, Microsoft and GitLab agree on a core that a skill can enforce. Address the reader as "you." Use active voice that names the actor and present tense for general behavior ("The server sends," not "will send") ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/highlights); [Google: tense](https://developers.google.com/style/tense)). Headings are sentence case with no end punctuation ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/top-10-tips-style-voice)). Put conditions before instructions ("If you use a proxy, set…") ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/highlights)). Procedures follow a strict pattern. Introduce them with a full sentence ("To customize the buttons, follow these steps:"). Number the steps, one action each, each opening with an imperative verb. Put the location and the goal before the action ("In **Settings**, tap…"). Give the result or reason after the action in the same step. Use no directional words such as "above" or "right-hand side". Offer only the best method. Introduce a command by saying what it does, not "run the following command" ([Google: procedures](https://developers.google.com/style/procedures); [Microsoft: procedures](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/procedures-instructions/writing-step-by-step-instructions)). UI labels go in bold, matched exactly, as the object of a verb, without the element type: "Tap **Save**," not 'Click the "Save" button' ([Google: UI elements](https://developers.google.com/style/ui-elements)). Use "tap" on touch devices, without "on" ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/guide/applestyleguide/t-apsg841c3645/web)), and "select" where the input device varies ([GitLab](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/styleguide/word_list/)).

The guides also agree on words to cut. "Simply," "easy" and "just" cost trust when the task is not easy for the reader. "Please," "note that," "in order to," "currently," "e.g.", "i.e." and "once" meaning "after" are filler or ambiguous. Modal verbs carry fixed meanings: "must" for required, "should" for recommended, "can" for optional, "might" for possibility, and "may" only for permission ([Google word list](https://developers.google.com/style/word-list); [GitLab word list](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/styleguide/word_list/)). GitLab enforces these with Vale lint rules, which shows they can be checked by machine ([GitLab](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/styleguide/word_list/)). Federal plain-language guidance adds three more rules. Turn hidden verbs back into verbs ("We conduct an analysis" becomes "We analyze"). Express one idea per sentence. Keep paragraphs to about 150 words, never over 250, each with a topic sentence ([Digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing); [Digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/clear-short)). Readability formulas only flag text that is too hard. They cannot judge familiarity or cohesion, and they penalize lists that help readers ([AHRQ](https://www.ahrq.gov/talkingquality/resources/writing/tip6.html); [UXmatters](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2019/07/readability-formulas-7-reasons-to-avoid-them-and-what-to-do-instead.php)). The often-quoted figures on sentence length and comprehension (8 words fully understood, 14 words 90%) trace to an American Press Institute study that was never published ([PRSA](https://prsay.prsa.org/2009/01/14/how-to-make-your-copy-more-readable-make-sentences-shorter/)), so length caps should be review flags, not limits.

ASD-STE100 makes the same point from aerospace maintenance manuals. Its transferable rules are concrete: **at most 20 words in a procedural sentence and 25 in a descriptive one**, one instruction per sentence in the imperative, one meaning and one part of speech per word, and no present perfect ("We have received" becomes "We received") ([Wikipedia: STE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Technical_English); [TechScribe](https://techscribe.co.uk/techw/asd-simplified-technical-english.htm)). Its approved dictionary is where it stops helping. The dictionary lacks everyday software verbs, and its synonym ban works against readers who search with synonyms. Even STE practitioners break that rule on purpose, and Barry Rosenberg argues for keeping technical nouns fixed while letting general verbs vary ([TechScribe](https://techscribe.co.uk/techw/asd-simplified-technical-english.htm)). The STE standard itself was not read for this report (it requires registration), so its rules on phrasal verbs, noun clusters and warnings are unverified here.

| STE rule | Use in software docs | How a skill applies it |
|---|---|---|
| One meaning per word, no synonyms | Keep, for the project's own nouns | A glossary of canonical terms ("favorite", "the seal"). The common search synonym appears once, then the canonical term only |
| One part of speech per word | Keep for technical terms | A term used as a noun ("the queue") is not also a verb in the same doc |
| 20 words (procedure), 25 words (description) | Keep as flags | Flag longer sentences in review, and split those that hold two ideas |
| One instruction per sentence, imperative | Keep | One action per numbered step |
| No present perfect | Keep | "nori saved the queue," not "nori has saved" |
| Approved general dictionary only | Drop | Allow ordinary software verbs (open, sign in, sync) |
| Monotonous uniformity everywhere | Relax on explanation pages | Varied general verbs and some analogy in explanation, strict wording in steps and reference |

## Tables, diagrams and notices each have a narrow job

Each layout element has a test for when it earns its place. Use a numbered list for steps in order, a bulleted list for parallel items, prose for reasoning, and a table only for **two-dimensional data**: items that share three or more attributes a reader will compare ([Google: tables](https://developers.google.com/style/tables); [Microsoft: tables](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/tables)). Never use a one-column table, a single-row table outside reference, a table in the middle of a procedure, or a table used for layout ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/tables); [GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/table/)). Inside a table, the row identifier goes in the left column, entries in a column are parallel, cells hold "ideally one line," headers are specific ("Employee", not "Name") and in sentence case, and no cell is blank or holds only a dash ("None" or "Not applicable") ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/tables)). Don't merge cells. Sort rows logically, or alphabetically when no order exists. Introduce the table with a complete sentence, because not every screen reader announces tables ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/tables)). Right-align numbers that will be compared ([GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/table/)). A practical test settles most cases: if a cell needs a second sentence, the content belongs in prose under a heading. A yes/no matrix such as feature by client uses words or symbols with a text equivalent, never color alone ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/tables)).

Diagrams have a precise justification. Larkin and Simon showed that a diagram pays off by indexing information by location and supporting perceptual inference. They also showed that this does not "insure that an arbitrary diagram is worth 10,000 of any set of words" ([Larkin & Simon](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf)). A diagram belongs where the spatial layout itself carries information. Use a sequence diagram for messages between parties over time, a state diagram for modes and transitions, a flowchart for decisions the reader must follow, and a box diagram for which component talks to which. Linear steps are better as a numbered list. C4's notation rules make box diagrams honest. Give every diagram a title stating its type and scope and a key for shapes, colors and line styles. Label each element with its responsibility and technology. Make each arrow one-directional and labeled with its intent, never a bare "Uses". Check the diagram in black and white and for color blindness ([C4 notation](https://c4model.com/diagrams/notation)). Google adds that a figure introduces no information the text lacks ([Google: images](https://developers.google.com/style/images)), and W3C requires a long description of the relationships, available to everyone, for any complex image ([W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/)).

Keep diagrams as code so they change in the same commit as the system they draw. GitHub renders `mermaid` code blocks natively in Markdown files ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/writing-on-github/working-with-advanced-formatting/creating-diagrams)), and Mermaid's `accTitle` and `accDescr` add an accessible title and description to the SVG, but only when the author writes them ([Mermaid](https://mermaid.js.org/config/accessibility.html)). Starlight has no built-in Mermaid support ([Starlight discussion #1259](https://www.github.com/withastro/starlight/discussions/1259)). The norifm.com site runs `@astrojs/starlight` 0.42 with no Mermaid integration in its `package.json`, so a Mermaid block there would show as code until a plugin such as astro-mermaid (client-side, follows the theme) is added ([astro-mermaid](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/astro-mermaid@2.1.0/README.md)). Developer docs in `docs/developers/` render Mermaid on GitHub today. Either way, the prose next to the diagram must stand on its own. That covers screen readers, unrendered blocks and ASCII diagrams, which screen readers read character by character.

Notices (Starlight's asides) are the element writers overuse most. Google's rules are blunt. Write the content as normal text first and only then decide whether it needs a notice. Too many notices "lose their visual distinctiveness," especially two in a row, and "readers skip elements on the page, including notices." So a notice never holds a prerequisite, a step, an expected result, a cross-reference, or anything required to succeed. A warning is reserved for actions that are irreversible or that can cost money, work or security ([Google: notices](https://developers.google.com/style/notices)). Screenshots follow the same economy: only UI that matters to the step, cropped, consistent across the doc set, PII covered with an opaque box, and never a picture of text, code or terminal output ([Google: images](https://developers.google.com/style/images)). For an app whose UI changes often, the exact label in bold does the job a screenshot would, without the upkeep. A screenshot is worth adding only when position or appearance cannot be put into words.

## AI-written docs fail on density and truth before vocabulary

Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" catalog names the root cause in one line: LLMs "regress toward generic, statistically common statements, replacing specific facts with vague, inflated ones" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Its tells sort into habits a docs skill can name. The first is inflated significance: "serves as a crucial component," "plays a pivotal role," "ensuring seamless". The second is trailing "-ing" clauses that pretend to analyze ("highlighting," "ensuring," "reflecting"). Others are avoiding plain "is" and "has" ("serves as," "boasts," "features"), negative parallelisms that correct a misconception nobody held ("It's not X, it's Y," and in newer models "Y rather than X"), padded triads, and synonym cycling. More are "it's important to note" hedges, closing "In summary" paragraphs, title-case headings, bold-term inline-header lists ("- **Retries:** The client retries…"), emoji, and leftover chat phrases ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Corpus work confirms the vocabulary shift. In 14 million PubMed abstracts, "delves" appeared **28 times** more often in 2024 than the pre-LLM trend predicted, "underscores" 13.8 times and "showcasing" 10.7 times. 66% of the excess style words were verbs ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5)).

The same catalog warns against stopping at vocabulary. The tells shift with each model generation. Em dashes are now "historical," and recent models instead overuse short sentences, periods and commas. Fixing surface signs "isn't enough" and only makes the text harder to detect ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). The slop study gives the stronger reason. When people label text as slop, relevance, information density and tone predict the label best, and zero-shot LLM judges (GPT-5, DeepSeek-V3, o3-mini) performed near chance at spotting it ([Shaib et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19163v2)). A skill that only bans words will produce clean-sounding, empty pages. A model reviewing its own draft for "sounds like AI" is not a reliable check. The rule that holds across model eras is that every sentence carries a fact the reader needs for their task.

Docs practitioners describe failures that no word list catches. Winland names several. The model over-explains what any practitioner knows. It repeats the same prerequisites in the intro, setup and troubleshooting. It drifts between `config.yaml`, "the configuration file" and "your settings file". It shows false completeness, detailed on easy parameters and silent on edge cases and failures ("Coverage without prioritization isn't technical writing, it's an index"). It puts context before action. It writes sections that make sense only after the previous one. And it treats length as quality ([Winland](https://vwinland.github.io/blog/ai-wrote-your-docs)). Ferri-Benedetti adds the more dangerous ones. Hallucinated commands read plausibly ("Who is accountable when a document tells you to run a specific command that doesn't exist?"). AI drafts have no view of what to leave out, and no product truth: docs must also say "what you cannot do or what you shouldn't be doing" ([passo.uno](https://passo.uno/whats-wrong-ai-generated-docs/)). Auto-generated code wikis capture the what and how from the code and miss the why, which he calls "documentation theater" ([I'd Rather Be Writing](https://idratherbewriting.com/blog/podcast-episode-3-documentation-theater-acceleration-paradox)). Restating code line by line is the developer-docs version of the same failure.

Over-correcting produces the opposite failure. Compressed "LLMese", with articles, verbs and connectives dropped and private hyphenated compounds coined, saves tokens but loses the relations between facts. In Ferri-Benedetti's test, meaning-preserving terse rewrites kept QA scores with 25 to 51% token savings. Aggressive pruning saved about 68% and "keeps the entities but drops the relationships between them," which lost facts. His advice: "Write for humans, then generate and test machine-oriented versions from that source" ([passo.uno](https://passo.uno/write-docs-llms-language/)). The fix for both failures is the same. Write full sentences that state relations ("The cache evicts the least recently used track when it passes 256 MB"), and define each coined term once or replace it with a plain description. Swapping punctuation to dodge a tell (every dash turned into a period) only creates the next tell.

The remedies writers report converge. Give the model the audience, the reader's task, the page type and the real source material, and tell it to use only that material. Anchor the voice with a few existing human-written pages rather than a list of adjectives. Check every response for factual errors, and edit a nearly-right draft by hand instead of re-prompting ([Google Tech Writing](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/two/llms); [passo.uno](https://passo.uno/ai-tech-writer-examples/index.md)). Ask explicitly for errors, edge cases and failure states ([Winland](https://vwinland.github.io/blog/ai-wrote-your-docs)). Compare the draft against the source and list every conflicting statement ([Tom Johnson](https://idratherbewriting.com/blog/error-checking-the-output-prompt-engineering)). GitLab also reviews AI output for repetition, unverifiable claims, and wrong scope, such as a new page for one concept ([GitLab](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/styleguide/)).

## The rules a docs-writing skill should encode, in working order

The research supports a skill that works in four phases: decide, gather, write, verify. Most of its value is in the first two and the last. The table below states each rule with its source in the sections above. "Mechanical" marks rules a grep or lint can check, which matters because model self-review is unreliable.

| Phase | Rule | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Decide | Name the reader, what they already know, and the task or question they arrive with | Written down before drafting |
| Decide | Classify the page with the two Diátaxis questions. One type per page; sentences from another type become links | Review |
| Decide | User page, explanation page or developer page: never all three. A user page links one level down to "How X works" | Review |
| Gather | Read the code or product and list each fact (command, path, default, limit, error message, client availability) with where it came from | Every fact has a source line |
| Gather | Collect the hard parts first: failure states, errors with their fixes, what the feature cannot do, costs | At least one per feature page |
| Write | First one or two sentences say what the page lets the reader do or know, and for whom, plus prerequisites (client, version, server) | Review |
| Write | Title by type: verb phrase for tasks, the thing's name for reference, a "How X works" phrase for explanation. Sentence case, no end punctuation | Mechanical |
| Write | Headings, list items and links start with their meaningful words. Siblings never share their first words | Mechanical (first-word duplicates) |
| Write | Link text names the destination. No "here," "learn more," bare URLs, or "as described above" | Mechanical |
| Write | Steps: full-sentence intro, numbered, one imperative action each, location and goal first, result after, recovery line after any step that can fail | Review |
| Write | UI labels exact and bold as the object of the verb; "tap" on touch clients, "click" or "select" on desktop | Review |
| Write | "You," active voice with a named actor, present tense, simple past instead of present perfect, contractions allowed | Mechanical (passive, "has been") |
| Write | One canonical term per concept from a project glossary, and one home per fact. Elsewhere, link to it | Mechanical (glossary variants) |
| Write | Sentences over 20 words in steps or 25 in descriptions are flagged and split when they hold two ideas. Paragraphs under about 150 words | Mechanical flag |
| Write | Full sentences with articles and connectives. Every coined compound defined once or replaced | Review |
| Write | Table only for items with three or more compared attributes. Identifier column first, one-line cells, no blanks, intro sentence | Review |
| Write | Diagram only when layout carries information. Mermaid source, title, key, labeled one-way arrows, prose equivalent beside it | Review |
| Write | Notices rare, never two in a row, never holding a required step. Warnings only for data loss, money, security or irreversible actions | Mechanical (adjacent asides) |
| Write | No "currently," "new," future promises, or history; the product as it is today | Mechanical |
| Cut | Banned filler: simply, easy, just, please, note that, in order to, e.g./i.e., once (meaning "after"), above/below, allows you to, it's important to note | Mechanical |
| Cut | AI habits: significance clauses ("crucial," "pivotal," "ensuring…," "highlighting…"), "serves as" for "is," strawman contrasts, padded triads, closing summaries, intros that restate the title, inline-header bold lists, emoji, bold inside running prose | Mechanical plus review |
| Verify | Run or trace every command, path, flag, default and label against the source or the running app. Cut anything unverified | Required |
| Verify | Compare the draft against the gathered facts and list conflicts; for each sentence, ask what the reader loses if it is deleted | Review |

Two cautions apply to the table. The vocabulary rows date quickly, because the tells change with each model generation, so the habit-level rows (inflation, strawman contrast, synonym cycling, summary endings) matter more than any word. And none of the numbers (20 words, 150 words, two disclosure levels) comes from a controlled study of software documentation. They are style-guide consensus and general web-reading data, useful as alarms. Nothing in the notes measured page length, heading depth or notice count in software docs specifically, and no study compared tables, lists and prose for comprehension.

## Conclusion

Most of the research points away from where docs-writing advice usually focuses. Style rules decide whether a page reads cleanly. What decides whether it helps is what the writer chose to include. Readers reward pages that put the hard part first (the failure, the cost, the thing the feature will not do), and AI drafts fail on exactly that part, because a model given code and no reader produces even coverage. A docs skill therefore gets more from forcing a fact-gathering step with sources, and a final question of "what would the reader lose without this sentence," than from any list of banned words. The banned list is still worth running mechanically, because it costs nothing, but it does not measure quality.

For nori in particular, several pieces are already in place: docs change in the same commit as the code, the Layout table is a codemap, and the Boundaries section lists invariants. The highest-value additions follow from the evidence. First, an architecture page that traces one tap on a song through every layer. Second, user feature pages with a fixed shape that link once to a "How it works" page. Third, a project glossary that makes STE's one-term rule concrete (favorite and seal, never heart). Fourth, a decision about Mermaid on the Starlight site before the first architecture diagram goes there.
